1 · Arrive

The alarm at 3 AM

A system built to demand instant trust — a smoke alarm, a nuclear early-warning system — makes hesitation feel unbearable, because the cost of hesitating during a real emergency is so much worse than a false alarm. This chapter is about the handful of real people who paused anyway.

Privacy: Section 7 asks about a real moment when a system pushed you toward instant action. Use a real one if comfortable, or keep it general.

Please check this box before continuing.

Before You Start Typing

The boxes in this workbook are graded responses, not a warm-up. A thin answer restates the obvious. A full answer names something specific and traceable, not a general truth that could apply to almost anything.

For example: asked why a chess player lost a won position, a thin answer says "they made a mistake." A full answer says "with 40 seconds left on the clock, they moved instinctively to a familiar-looking pattern that actually walked into a forced mate in three, missing it purely from time pressure, not lack of skill." Both are about the same length. Only one of them could actually be checked against what happened.

Each prompt below has a short note under it naming the specific shortcut a rushed answer tends to take on that exact question. Read it before you write, not after you get your grade back.

2 · Stanislav Petrov

September 26, 1983

Petrov, a Soviet lieutenant colonel on duty at a nuclear early-warning center, watched the system report five incoming American missiles. He judged the pattern looked more like a technical malfunction than a real attack, and reported it as a false alarm. He was right — sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds had confused the satellite. For decades, almost no one outside a small circle knew how close the world had come.

He was working from an incomplete, possibly wrong picture, under enormous pressure to trust the system completely. What let him pause anyway?

Watch for: saying "he was brave" or "he trusted his instincts" — those are personality labels, not an explanation. A full answer names the specific piece of reasoning or knowledge that let him judge the pattern didn't match a real attack, not just that he felt confident.

3 · Vasili Arkhipov

October 27, 1962

During the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Soviet submarine B-59 was cut off from Moscow and being depth-charged by American forces. The crew believed war might already be underway. Launching the submarine's nuclear torpedo required agreement from three senior officers. Two agreed. Arkhipov, the third, refused.

Arkhipov had even less information than Petrov, and his refusal ran directly against his own fellow officers in the room. What does that add to the case?

Watch for: restating that "more people disagreed with him" — that's the premise already given, not an answer. A full answer names what specifically became harder about the judgment itself when it required refusing two colleagues in the same room, not just reasoning alone at a console.

This still needs a response.

4 · Visible Stability, Hidden Fragility

Decades of stability was not evidence the system was safe

The entire nuclear standoff looked stable for decades. What that stability was actually hiding: the system survived not because its design was flawless, but because on at least two occasions, a specific person made a judgment call the system itself was not built to allow for.

Identify an institution or system you're part of that looks stable from the outside. What is one piece of evidence its stability might be hiding something rather than reflecting genuine health?

Watch for: naming an institution and asserting it's fragile without naming one specific, checkable piece of evidence — a near-miss, a complaint that got buried, unusual turnover. A full answer points to something concrete you could actually describe to someone else, not just a general feeling that something's off.

This still needs a response.

5 · The Adaptive Moral Commonwealth

Named, at last

The direction a group, institution, or civilization moves in when it has genuinely learned to coordinate across genuine disagreement — not by requiring shared values, but by building the conditions under which people who disagree about almost everything can still detect danger, repair mistakes, and preserve each other's capacity to keep contributing.

Why are Petrov and Arkhipov exactly what an Adaptive Moral Commonwealth actually depends on — not a perfect system, but what?

Watch for: calling them "heroes" — that's praise, not an answer to what the Adaptive Moral Commonwealth structurally depends on. A full answer names the specific thing their actions supplied that the system's own design couldn't: what a perfect system cannot build in on its own.

This still needs a response.

6 · The Civilizational Chiliagon

Harder than the original

A chiliagon, however large, is one static object with no mind of its own. Civilizational cooperation is an uncountable number of independent agents, each capable of changing their own mind for reasons no outside observer could fully predict.

Not just a bigger version of the chiliagon — why is it a genuinely different kind of problem?

Watch for: answering "because there are more people" or "it's bigger" — the passage already tells you scale isn't the point. A full answer explains what changes when the objects involved can independently think and change their minds, not just increase in number.

This still needs a response.

7 · Before You Reason It Through

A moment a system pushed you toward instant action

Stop — write before continuing

Recall a real moment in your own life where a system, a rule, or an authority pushed you toward instant action, and you paused anyway to check. Write your honest first sense of what that pause cost you. Once you move on, don't come back and revise this.

Watch for: describing the moment itself without naming what the pause actually cost you — time, someone's trust, your own standing in that moment. The prompt specifically asks for the cost, not just the story. And if you've already justified whether the pause was worth it, that's not a first reaction.

This still needs a response.

8 · Apply the Capacity Check to Petrov

All things considered, given his actual state

Applying the deepened Capacity Check from Chapter 7 as best you can reconstruct it: what would "all things considered, given my actual state right now" have looked like for a person in Petrov's exact position?

Watch for: describing how Petrov must have felt — "he was probably terrified" — instead of reconstructing the actual check. A full answer names what specific factors, such as his training, his fatigue, or the time pressure, the Capacity Check would have had to weigh against each other.

This still needs a response.

9 · After Working Through It

Your final answer

Stop — write before continuing

Having thought through Petrov's and Arkhipov's cases, was your own pause worth it? Was it, in the end, the right call?

Watch for: a bare yes or no, or restating your Section 7 account with more conviction. A full answer says specifically what thinking through Petrov's and Arkhipov's cases changed or confirmed about how you judge your own pause now.

10 · The Revision Check

What actually changed?

Name one specific, concrete thing that changed. If nothing changed, say so honestly and explain why your first reaction already held up.

Watch for: writing "nothing changed" and stopping there. That's an allowed outcome, but only when you explain specifically why your first reaction survived being tested against Petrov and Arkhipov's cases, not just assert that it did.
